About Me

Dr. Sahaya Vijay Jeyaraj
Postdoctoral Researcher, NIMS

I am an interdisciplinary researcher with a strong background in applied mathematics, chemical kinetics, and chemical reaction networks. Currently, I am a postdoctoral researcher at the National Institute for Materials Science (NIMS), Japan, where I develop accurate mathematical models and analytical formulations to predict the properties and dynamics of complex, nonlinear chemical systems.

My doctoral research was in chemical graph theory, focusing on predicting the physicochemical properties of metamaterials and drug-related structures. The central contribution of that work was using regression analysis to evaluate a wide range of topological indices and identify those with the strongest predictive power for the physicochemical properties of several important classes of molecular structures.

Across both my doctoral and postdoctoral work, a single thread runs through everything: the analysis of chemical reaction networks. I am passionate about leveraging mathematical tools to gain deeper insight into chemical processes and to contribute to advances in materials science and related disciplines.

The greatest discoveries are made at the boundaries between disciplines, where different ways of thinking come together to reveal new understanding.

Research Interests

My current postdoctoral research concerns predicting the kinetic properties of chemical reaction networks in champion materials, using mathematical modelling based on ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and graph theory. Although the systems and fields differ, all of my projects rest on the same core framework: the analysis of chemical reaction networks.

Chemical Graph Theory

I represent molecules and crystals as graphs and design fast algorithms to describe their structure. These algorithms give counting polynomials and distance-based descriptors for boric acid, aluminophosphates and fullerene cages. I relate these descriptors to the NMR and ESR spectral patterns of boric acid nanosheets. I then use them in quantitative structure–property relationship (QSPR) and machine-learning models. These models predict the properties of drugs and graphene nanoribbons.

Autocatalysis & the Origin of Life

How did the first chemical networks begin to amplify themselves? I study this by writing protometabolism in an open system as a reaction network of ordinary differential equations (ODEs). From this model I derived the rate at which autocatalysis amplifies itself in linear networks. This rate shows when a catalytic network grows and keeps itself alive, and when it fades away. A manuscript on this result is under minor revision at Physical Review E. This gives a clear kinetic test for self-sustaining chemistry.

Mathematical & Computational Modelling

Across all my projects, I turn a reaction network into equations that I can solve and interpret. I developed methods that estimate how long an autocatalytic network survives. I also separate the dynamics of linear networks into their individual timescales. These methods rest on linear algebra, stability analysis and timescale analysis. I implement and test them in Python and Mathematica, and apply them to the origin of life and electrochemistry. These tools let me compare different systems in one framework.

Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy

An impedance spectrum carries the kinetics of every step in a catalytic reaction. I build analytical microkinetic models that read this kinetic information for multi-step reactions. By solving the kinetic ODEs, I obtained a formula for the surface coverage. From it I derived a new equation for fitting electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data. The resulting impedance spectra and Bode plots connect directly to the kinetic timescales of a catalyst. I also simulate and fit spectra numerically.

AI-Assisted Research & Development

I use AI tools as a regular part of my research and software work. I direct agentic coding tools such as Claude Code to build programs from specifications that I write. I built a no-code graphical tool this way, and it is now under peer review. I defined every algorithm and feature in it myself. I also use AI assistants to draft and review code, derivations and manuscripts. I never accept an output unchecked. I validate each one against analytical results, numerical tests or known reference values.

Scientific Computing & Open Tools

I build open software that makes molecular descriptors easy to compute and check. PyTIE is a Python program of four packages. It calculates degree-based topological descriptors and molecular entropy from molecular graphs, and its code is on GitHub. The method is described in an arXiv preprint. PyTIE automates calculations that are slow and error-prone by hand, so large sets of molecules can be analysed quickly. Because the code is open, other researchers can reuse it, check it and extend it.
